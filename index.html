Document Intelligence at Scale

Waterworth · 2025–present

Turning large archives of unstructured municipal records, much of it scanned paper with no text layer, into structured, queryable data.

The problem

Government records are close to a worst-case document corpus: decades of scanned paper, inconsistent layouts, and legal text where a missed entity isn't a rounding error, it's a wrong answer. Clients want to query all of it like a database. Off-the-shelf OCR gives you characters, not meaning. A single LLM pass over noisy OCR will hallucinate confidently enough to be genuinely dangerous in a regulatory context.

Why this problem class is hard

Everything starts as pixels. Scanned records have no text layer, so the pipeline can't begin at the language model. It has to begin with layout understanding and work upward. Errors there propagate to everything downstream.

Institutional documents love borderless tables. Rows separated by whitespace alone defeat parsers that key on ruling lines, so table structure has to be recovered from geometry rather than read off the page.

Partial credit doesn't exist. An entity attributed to the wrong section is worse than no answer, because it looks correct. That shapes everything about how you evaluate the system: recall alone is a misleading number, and confident wrong answers are the failure mode worth engineering against.

What I own

I authored and own the platform end to end: the document-vision layer that turns scans into structured regions and text, the extraction layer that turns that into typed data, the validation layer that decides how much of it to trust, and the retrieval APIs that serve semantic search and RAG on top. Alongside it, the orchestration that lets non-technical domain experts run client data ingest themselves with no engineer in the loop, and the evaluation harness and release gates that catch extraction regressions before they reach production rather than after a client notices a wrong answer.

The outcome I care most about isn't an accuracy number. It's that the people who understand the documents can now operate the system without me.

Multimodal Audio Understanding

Avid Technology · 2024–2025

Making large professional audio libraries searchable and conversational in natural language.

The problem

Audio libraries are effectively opaque. Filenames and manual tags are all most systems have to go on, which means the thing you need is findable only if someone already described it the way you're about to search for it. The goal was to let people ask for what they wanted in plain language and get the right audio back.

Why it's hard

Audio has no natural index. Text search works because text is already symbols. Audio is a waveform until a model decides otherwise, so retrieval quality is bounded by how good your encoder is before a single query is ever run.

The ground truth is subjective. Whether a tag "sounds right" is a judgement call, which means the evaluation metrics have to be defined before the model can be said to improve at all. I defined and tracked those metrics, so the 19% is measured against them, not against a vibe.

Annotation-Efficient Segmentation for Underwater Acoustics

ASL Environmental Sciences · 2022–2024

Deep learning on Arctic and Pacific echograms, where the bottleneck was never compute but expert time. In alliance with Mitacs and NSERC Canada.

The problem

Echograms are read by marine biologists, and there are very few of them. Every labelled example costs scarce expert attention, so the usual answer of labelling more data simply wasn't available. The real question was how to get good segmentation out of annotations that were naive, sparse, or partially wrong.

Why it's hard

You cannot buy your way out with data. Most segmentation results assume labels are cheap. Here the labelling budget was the binding constraint, which pushes the problem away from architecture search and toward learning strategy.

Acoustic boundaries are fuzzy. Ocean boundary phenomena don't have crisp edges the way objects in photographs do, so generic segmentation losses optimise for the wrong thing, hence boundary-aware and class-aware loss functions built for the structure of the data rather than borrowed from natural images.

Approach

A two-step model combining image classification with semantic segmentation, using boundary loss functions and class-aware losses designed around acoustic data. Semi-automatic labelling algorithms generated ground truth that experts corrected rather than authored, turning the expensive step into a review task. U-Net, DeepLab, InceptionV3, and ResNet variants were trained and compared on CUDA GPUs across large multi-frequency datasets. The work became four peer-reviewed publications and a master's thesis.

Real-Time Fish Detection on Fishing Video

Archipelago Marine Research · 2023

Computer vision for electronic monitoring of long-line fisheries, running on real-world vessel footage, complete with salt spray, bad light, and a moving deck.

What actually mattered

Built real-time detection reaching 84% accuracy on long-line fishing video. The instructive part: the biggest gains didn't come from the model. Leading the iterative development of the annotation system feeding it raised data collection efficiency by 70% and drove most of the accuracy improvement. I also owned the MLOps side: data pipelines, model versioning, and deployment through Azure Machine Learning, MLflow, and CI/CD.

It's the clearest example I have of a lesson that keeps recurring in this work: the leverage is usually upstream of the model.
